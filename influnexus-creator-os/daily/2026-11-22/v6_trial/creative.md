## Script variants

### Version A: curiosity storytelling (SELECTED)

93 words, about 42.7 s

```
Seventeen million lines of code might just settle a 167-year-old math problem.

It's the Riemann Hypothesis, the most famous open question about prime numbers.

On November 20, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge said he and Claude had proved it.

But this proof isn't written for people. It's Lean code that a computer can check.

A day later, Lean's own maintainers said they have full confidence it's correct.

The strange part? Even they say the next job is for mathematicians to explain why it works.

So we may have the answer before anyone understands it.
```

### Version B: practical usefulness

96 words, about 43.6 s

```
An AI-assisted proof of math's most famous problem just passed a computer's check.

On November 20, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge said he and Claude had proved the Riemann Hypothesis. It's a 167-year-old question about prime numbers.

The proof is about 17 million lines of Lean, code that a computer can check.

A day later, Lean's own maintainers said they have full confidence it's correct.

That's a useful habit for all of us. When AI hands you an answer, ask for something you can check.

Even they say the next job is human: explaining why it works.
```

### Version C: surprising consequences

90 words, about 41.4 s

```
Math's most famous problem may be solved, and nobody can explain why yet.

The Riemann Hypothesis has been open for 167 years. It's about how prime numbers behave.

On November 20, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge said he and Claude had proved it, in about 17 million lines of Lean code.

A day later, Lean's own maintainers said they have full confidence it's correct.

Even they say the next job is for mathematicians to explain why it works.

That's new. The answer arrived before the understanding. So who gets the credit?
```

**Why A:** A opens on the most visual, concrete hook (17 million lines), builds a clear story with a twist and ends on a memorable line; B's 'ask for something you can check' lesson feels bolted on, and C opens on an abstract claim that is harder to show.

## Creative quality gate

- Would a nontechnical viewer care? **YES**: Everyone has heard of 'unsolved math problems'; an AI-assisted proof that only a computer can check is a story anyone can retell.
- Is the hook genuinely compelling? **YES**: A concrete, visual number (17 million lines) against a 167-year-old mystery creates an immediate curiosity gap in 13 words.
- Does the story deliver on the hook? **YES**: The script names the problem, who claims the proof, how it was checked and who certified it, all within the first 30 seconds.
- Is there a surprising or useful takeaway? **YES**: The surprising implication: we may have a verified answer before any human understands why it is true.
- Is the language natural? **YES**: Short spoken sentences, no jargon beyond 'Lean', which is explained in the same line.
- Can the story be visualized? **YES**: Real Lean code is public on GitHub to scroll, and the creator can record a tiny Lean proof being checked.
- Are the claims verified? **YES**: Every script claim passes the tiered gate; the certification and Alpöge's claim are attributed in the spoken words.
- Is the ending memorable? **YES**: 'So we may have the answer before anyone understands it' is a quotable, debatable closing line.
- Does the script sound like a real creator? **YES**: Conversational asides ('The strange part?') and a first-person storyteller tone, not a news-anchor read.
